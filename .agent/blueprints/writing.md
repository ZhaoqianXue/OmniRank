# OmniRank: A Large-Language-Model Agent Platform for Statistically Rigorous Ranking Inference from Arbitrary Multiway Comparisons

## Abstract

Ranking from multiway comparison data is central to many real-world applications, from benchmarking large language models and sports competitors to prioritizing treatments and biological stimuli. Spectral ranking inference offers a statistically rigorous and computationally efficient way to analyze such data while quantifying uncertainty, but its linear algebra-based implementation remains difficult for most domain experts to use in practice. In this study, we present OmniRank, an agentic framework that synergizes the reasoning capabilities of Large Language Models (LLMs) with the mathematical rigor of spectral ranking inference. Unlike standard LLMs that are prone to hallucinations in arithmetic tasks, OmniRank decouples instruction following from computation: an LLM controller parses user queries and data, delegating the rigorous inference to a specialized Spectral Calculation Engine. Case study results on both synthetic and real-world datasets show that OmniRank achieves accurate ranking recovery comparable to established statistical packages. By providing an interactive, no-code interface for spectral ranking, OmniRank broadens access to rigorous ranking analysis and uncertainty inference for researchers across the social and natural sciences.

## 1 Introduction

Ranking inferences from comparison data are ubiquitous in scientific inquiry and modern applications, ranging from identifying optimal treatments in clinical trials and ranking biological stimuli to evaluating the relative strength of competitors in sports and gaming [1, 2]. While classical frameworks like the Bradley-Terry-Luce model have successfully handled pairwise comparisons, real-world data increasingly manifest as multiway comparisons, where multiple items are compared simultaneously—such as in horse races, multi-player online games, or top-k choice data in econometrics [3, 4]. Unlike pairwise data, multiway comparisons involve hyperedges of heterogeneous sizes, creating complex dependency structures that defy simple aggregation. Although the Plackett-Luce model offers a probabilistic foundation for such data, its reliance on Maximum Likelihood Estimation (MLE) faces significant challenges: the likelihood function can be non-convex, and the computational burden becomes prohibitive as the number of items (n) grows, often requiring O(N^3) complexity for precise inference [5, 6].

To overcome these computational and statistical barriers, recent theoretical breakthroughs have established spectral ranking inferences based on general multiway comparisons as a superior alternative. By constructing a comparison graph where items are nodes and multiway comparisons form hyperedges, these methods utilize the stationary distribution of a random walk on the hypergraph (or the eigenvectors of the hypergraph Laplacian) to recover latent preference scores [7]. Fan et al. demonstrated that this spectral approach achieves minimax optimal statistical rates comparable to MLE but with significantly greater computational efficiency, even under heterogeneous sampling conditions where hyperedge sizes vary dramatically [8]. Despite this theoretical elegance, the practical application of spectral ranking remains confined to a small circle of statisticians. The implementation requires rigorous handling of sparse hypergraph adjacency matrices and complex linear algebra operations, creating a steep technical barrier for domain experts—such as sociologists or biologists—who possess rich multiway data but lack the coding expertise to implement these specialized spectral algorithms [9].

Large Language Models (LLMs) have emerged as potential intermediaries to democratize such advanced analytical tools. Models like GPT-4 have shown impressive capabilities in code generation and logical reasoning [10, 11]. However, standard LLMs inherently struggle with rigorous mathematical execution; they are prone to "hallucinations" when performing arithmetic or executing specific algorithms mentally, and they lack the native ability to process large-scale structured data (e.g., adjacency matrices) directly within their context window [12]. Consequently, the current wave of "AI Agents" has shifted towards a tool-use paradigm, where the LLM acts as a controller that delegates specific tasks to external computational tools [13, 14]. While agents have been developed for chemical synthesis [15] and gene analysis [16], there is currently no dedicated framework that bridges the gap between the sophisticated mathematics of spectral ranking and the intuitive needs of non-technical users.

Here, we introduce OmniRank, a novel web-based agentic framework that democratizes access to spectral ranking inferences. The architecture consists of two synergistic components: an LLM Agent that interprets user’s natural language requests and raw data uploads (e.g., “Rank these polygenic risk scores for breast cancer using their comparative AUC performance across the uploaded validation cohorts.”), and a Spectral Calculation Engine that executes the hypergraph construction and eigenvector computations. The results are then rendered through an interactive visualization dashboard, allowing users to explore ranking confidence intervals and topology without writing a single line of code. By decoupling the complex spectral inference (Backend) from the user interaction (Frontend), we ensure that the mathematical precision of the underlying theory is preserved while maximizing accessibility.

We validated OmniRank as a practical ranking tool through both controlled synthetic benchmarks and real-world application scenarios. On synthetic datasets with varying heterogeneity in comparison sizes (k), we tested how well the tool supports ranking analysis under different conditions. Furthermore, we demonstrate the tool’s practical utility on a real-world LLM evaluation dataset, where the agent successfully transformed raw comparison records into a complete ranking analysis. Our results show that by combining the reasoning power of LLMs with the mathematical rigor of spectral graph theory, we can effectively lower the barrier to entry for advanced statistical ranking, enabling broader application across diverse scientific fields.

## 2 Background and Related Works

The development of OmniRank draws upon two distinct research traditions: statistical methods for ranking inference from comparison data, and the emerging paradigm of LLM-based agents for scientific computing. This section reviews the relevant literature in both areas and positions OmniRank within the broader context of efforts to democratize advanced statistical methods.

### 2.1 Statistical Methods for Ranking from Comparison Data

The problem of inferring rankings from comparison data has a rich history spanning statistics, economics, and machine learning. The Bradley-Terry model [48] and its multiway extension, the Plackett-Luce model [46], have served as foundational frameworks for estimating latent preference scores from pairwise and multiway comparisons, respectively. These parametric models assume that comparison outcomes follow a logistic or multinomial logit distribution governed by item-specific quality parameters.

Classical inference for these models relies on Maximum Likelihood Estimation (MLE), which Hunter [4] showed can be efficiently computed via MM algorithms for the Bradley-Terry case. However, MLE approaches face significant computational challenges as the number of items grows: the likelihood function may be non-convex, and iterative optimization can require substantial computation time [5, 6]. These limitations have motivated the development of computationally efficient alternatives.

Spectral methods have emerged as a compelling solution to these computational bottlenecks. Rather than optimizing a likelihood function, spectral approaches construct a comparison graph where items correspond to nodes and comparisons induce edges, then extract rankings from the eigenvectors of an associated matrix [49]. Negahban et al. [7] demonstrated that spectral methods achieve statistically consistent estimates under the Bradley-Terry model with computational complexity dominated by a single eigenvalue computation. Shah and Wainwright [19] established that even simple counting-based algorithms like Borda count can achieve minimax optimal rates for pairwise ranking under certain conditions.

The theoretical understanding of spectral ranking has advanced considerably in recent years. Chen et al. [38] proved that spectral methods achieve the same optimal rates as regularized MLE for top-$K$ ranking problems while offering superior computational efficiency. Fan et al. [8] extended these results to the general multiway comparison setting, establishing minimax optimality of spectral methods for heterogeneous hypergraph structures. Despite these theoretical advances, the practical implementation of spectral ranking methods remains technically demanding, requiring careful handling of hypergraph Laplacians, eigenvector perturbation bounds, and bootstrap-based uncertainty quantification [41, 52].

### 2.2 Large Language Models as Scientific Agents

The rapid progress of large language models has opened new possibilities for automating scientific workflows [20]. Contemporary LLMs demonstrate impressive capabilities in code generation, logical reasoning, and natural language understanding, prompting researchers to explore their potential as "agents" capable of executing complex analytical tasks [21, 44]. In the data science domain, LLM-based systems have been deployed to automate data preprocessing, model selection, and result interpretation [22].

Several recent works have demonstrated the utility of LLM agents in specialized scientific domains. Boiko et al. [15] presented Coscientist, an agentic framework capable of autonomous chemical synthesis by integrating LLM reasoning with laboratory automation. Similarly, GeneAgent [16] employs self-verification mechanisms for gene-set analysis, while other systems have addressed clinical prediction [23] and materials discovery [24]. These applications share a common architectural pattern: the LLM serves as a cognitive controller that interprets user intent and orchestrates domain-specific computational tools.

A persistent challenge in deploying LLMs for quantitative analysis is their tendency to produce "hallucinations"---outputs that appear plausible but are factually incorrect or computationally erroneous [32]. This limitation is particularly acute for mathematical operations: LLMs can generate syntactically correct formulas that yield numerically wrong results, a failure mode that undermines their reliability for statistical inference [12]. The hallucination problem has motivated the tool-use paradigm [13], where LLMs delegate precise computations to external engines rather than attempting to execute algorithms internally.

The tool-use paradigm represents a fundamental shift in how LLMs are deployed for scientific computing. Systems like Toolformer [13] and TaskMatrix [14] demonstrate that LLMs can learn to invoke appropriate tools based on task requirements. However, most existing frameworks target general-purpose computation; specialized statistical methods like spectral ranking inference have received limited attention. OmniRank addresses this gap by integrating LLM-based data interpretation with a purpose-built spectral computation engine.

### 2.3 Single-Agent Tool Calling and Reliability

Recent work has increasingly focused on improving the reliability of LLM-based analytical systems through tool augmentation and structured execution. Toolformer [13] and ReAct [18] showed that language models can learn to interleave reasoning with external actions rather than rely solely on free-form text generation. PAL [55] extended this idea by translating natural language problems into executable Python programs, while Binder [56] connected language models to symbolic interfaces such as SQL and Python. Collectively, these studies suggest that reliability improves when the model is used primarily for task interpretation and action selection, and precise operations are delegated to deterministic runtimes.

This design principle has become particularly important in scientific and data-analysis settings, where errors often arise from multi-stage dependencies, context management, and unverifiable intermediate reasoning. Data Interpreter [22] introduced step-by-step verification and refinement for data science workflows, and GeneAgent [16] combined LLM reasoning with domain database checks to support gene-set analysis. Similarly, Coscientist [15] integrated language-model planning with external laboratory tools for chemical research. These systems indicate that reliable scientific agents typically depend on staged validation, external grounding, and explicit interfaces between reasoning and execution.

OmniRank is situated within this emerging line of work but focuses on spectral ranking inference, where numerical correctness and structural validity are especially critical. Rather than relying on free-form LLM generation for mathematical operations, OmniRank uses a single agent to interpret user intent and data semantics while delegating validation and spectral computation to dedicated tools. This architecture aligns the accessibility of natural-language interaction with the rigor required for graph-based ranking and bootstrap-based uncertainty quantification [8, 38].

## 3 Methodology

This section describes the methodological framework of OmniRank. Section 3.1 introduces the single-agent architecture and its tool registry. ### 3.1 Overview

OmniRank employs a single-agent tool-calling architecture in which the LLM handles semantic understanding---data interpretation, schema inference, natural-language synthesis---while all mathematical computation is delegated to deterministic external tools. This separation addresses a fundamental limitation of current LLMs: their unreliable performance on arithmetic and algorithmic tasks when executed purely in-model [32]. The same design principle underlies recent scientific agent systems that use LLMs as cognitive controllers while offloading precise computation to verified components [33, 34].

The system centers on a single LLM agent (gpt-5-mini) operating within one context window, equipped with a fixed registry of ten specialized tools organized into four categories:

- **Data Tools** (5): `read_data_file`, `infer_semantic_schema`, `validate_data_format`, `validate_data_quality`, and `preprocess_data`---used for data ingestion, schema inference, and validation against spectral ranking requirements.
- **User Interaction Tool** (1): `request_user_confirmation`---an explicit human-in-the-loop checkpoint for parameter verification.
- **Engine Tool** (1): `execute_spectral_ranking`---a deterministic tool that invokes the spectral computation engine in an isolated subprocess.
- **Analysis Tools** (3): `generate_report`, `generate_visualizations`, and `answer_question`---used for reporting, visualization, and interactive follow-up.

The agent orchestrates these tools through a fixed three-phase pipeline: (1) data processing and schema inference, (2) interactive configuration and spectral computation, and (3) result synthesis and user interaction. We adopt a single-agent design to keep reasoning and intermediate tool outputs within one context window and to avoid the coordination overhead of multi-agent architectures [35]. Tool calling ensures that precise numerical computation is delegated to verified deterministic tools rather than attempted by the LLM internally.

### 3.2 Data Processing and Schema Inference

The data processing phase converts heterogeneous user uploads into validated inputs for the spectral computation engine. OmniRank uses LLM-based semantic reasoning to infer a semantic schema and deterministic checks grounded in spectral ranking theory [37], rather than relying solely on unconstrained LLM code generation [36].

#### 3.2.1 Format Recognition and Validation

OmniRank classifies uploaded comparison data into one of two canonical formats---pairwise and multiway---based on the number of items participating in each observed comparison. This classification is operationally defined: it determines how the spectral computation engine decomposes each row into the sub-comparisons that populate the transition matrix $\mathbf{P}$ (Section 3.3.2), rather than reflecting an intrinsic property of the data source.

**Pairwise Format.** Each row records a head-to-head outcome between exactly two items, with a binary indicator denoting the winner (e.g., a user preference vote between two chatbot responses, or a tournament match result). Every observation corresponds to a choice set $A_l$ with $|A_l| = 2$, reducing the general Plackett-Luce model to the Bradley-Terry-Luce special case [48]. The engine processes each such row as a single comparison, contributing one pairwise observation to the transition matrix per row.

**Multiway Format.** Each row records an outcome among three or more items, expressed as a complete or partial ranking over the participants (e.g., finishing positions in a horse race, or performance metrics for competing methods evaluated on a common benchmark). Each observation corresponds to a choice set $A_l$ with $|A_l| \geq 3$. The engine applies an iterative elimination procedure: starting from the full participant set, the top-ranked item is identified, a sub-comparison among all current participants is recorded with the identified winner, and the winner is removed; this process repeats until a single item remains, yielding $|A_l| - 1$ sub-comparisons of decreasing group size per row [8]. This decomposition enables a unified spectral framework that accommodates heterogeneous comparison structures where choice-set sizes vary across observations.

Format recognition proceeds through a deterministic classifier applied to the data summary returned by `read_data_file`. After non-numeric and metadata columns are identified via the inferred semantic schema (Section 3.2.2), the classifier counts the number of non-missing numeric entries per row across the item columns. Rows with exactly two non-missing values are classified as pairwise; rows with three or more are classified as multiway. When all rows share the same item count, classification is unambiguous. When item counts vary across rows---as occurs in datasets with heterogeneous choice sets---the system defaults to multiway processing, since the iterative elimination procedure handles arbitrary set sizes including the $|A_l| = 2$ special case. For structurally ambiguous inputs where column semantics prevent confident automated classification, OmniRank surfaces the inferred format for user review during the interactive configuration phase (Section 3.3.1).

Following format recognition, OmniRank validates the data against theoretical requirements from the spectral ranking literature [8, 38]. Validation feedback is organized into three tiers that separate blocking errors from non-blocking warnings.

**Sparsity Assessment.** The system evaluates whether the comparison count $M$ satisfies the sample complexity bound $M \geq cn\log n$ for a constant $c > 0$, where $n$ denotes the number of items. This threshold, derived from the coupon-collector argument in Fan et al. [8], represents the minimum sample size for the spectral estimator to concentrate sufficiently for valid bootstrap-based inference. When $M / (n\log n) < 1$, OmniRank issues a non-blocking warning indicating that confidence intervals may be wider than theoretically optimal and should be interpreted with caution.

**Connectivity Verification.** A global ranking requires the comparison graph to form a single connected component---a necessary condition for the induced Markov chain to possess a unique stationary distribution [8, 49]. OmniRank constructs an undirected comparability graph in which items correspond to nodes and an edge connects any pair of items that co-occur in at least one comparison, then verifies connectivity via standard graph traversal. When the graph is disconnected, the system reports the number and composition of connected components and informs the user that cross-component rankings are not identifiable.

**Data Integrity Checks.** OmniRank verifies that the dataset contains at least two rankable items, that item columns are numeric or coercible to numeric type, and that comparison outcomes are non-degenerate (e.g., no rows in which all participating items share identical values). Datasets failing these structural checks are rejected with diagnostic feedback generated through LLM-based natural language synthesis.

This tiered validation architecture, illustrated in [Figure: Data validation workflow], separates critical errors that preclude computation (integrity failures, disconnected graphs) from non-blocking advisories (sparsity), enabling exploratory analyses on imperfect data while explicitly communicating theoretical limitations.

#### 3.2.2 Semantic Schema Inference

Alongside format classification (Algorithm 1, line 2), OmniRank infers a semantic schema $\mathcal{S}$ that binds data fields to the analysis primitives required by the spectral engine. The schema captures four fields---preference direction, ranking items, indicator column, and indicator values---reducing reliance on explicit parameter specification common in traditional statistical software.

**Preference Direction Inference.** The schema encodes whether higher metric values indicate superior performance (e.g., accuracy, win rate) or inferior performance (e.g., latency, error rate). OmniRank resolves this using a priority cascade: (i) lexical cues in column names (columns containing "accuracy" or "score" suggest higher-is-better; "time" or "error" suggest lower-is-better); (ii) distributional properties of the data (values bounded in $[0, 1]$ suggest a proportion metric with higher-is-better semantics); and (iii) optional user hints provided through the confirmation interface. Incorrect preference direction would invert the entire ranking, making this inference critical to downstream correctness.

**Entity and Indicator Extraction.** OmniRank identifies the ranking items (e.g., model names, player identifiers) and, when present, a categorical indicator column that partitions comparisons into semantically meaningful subgroups (e.g., task categories, evaluation conditions). When multiple candidate indicator columns exist, OmniRank selects at most one to maintain analytical focus, prioritizing columns with moderate cardinality (2--50 unique values) and clear semantic interpretation. The indicator column enables stratified analysis---producing per-subgroup rankings in addition to a global ranking---without requiring the user to pre-filter the dataset.

The complete inferred schema is surfaced in the interactive configuration phase (Section 3.3.1), where users can verify and modify any field before computation proceeds.

### 3.3 Interactive Configuration and Computation

This phase translates the inferred schema into statistical computation through two steps: explicit human confirmation of key parameters (Section 3.3.1) and deterministic spectral engine execution (Sections 3.3.2--3.3.3). The separation ensures that computation proceeds only after the user has verified the system's inferences, reflecting the tool-use paradigm in agentic AI where LLMs orchestrate but do not execute precise computations [39, 40].

#### 3.3.1 Human-in-the-Loop Configuration

Before computation, OmniRank presents the inferred schema $\mathcal{S}$ alongside validation results ($\mathcal{V}_f$, $\mathcal{V}_q$) through an interactive confirmation interface (Algorithm 1, line 8). Users confirm or revise four categories of parameters:

- **Preference direction**: Confirm or override the inferred interpretation of metric values (higher-is-better vs. lower-is-better).
- **Item selection**: Restrict analysis to a subset of items when the full set is not of interest.
- **Indicator selection**: Select which indicator values to include, enabling stratified analysis across subgroups.
- **Statistical parameters**: Configure bootstrap iterations $B$ (default: 2,000) and random seed (default: 42) for reproducibility.

This explicit checkpoint addresses a well-documented failure mode of fully automated agent systems: misalignment between inferred parameters and user intent [26, 27]. Schema inference may misidentify the preference direction when column names are semantically ambiguous (e.g., "rank" could indicate ordinal position or a score), and the indicator column selection may not match the user's analytical goal. By requiring explicit confirmation before computation, OmniRank ensures that the spectral engine operates on parameters the user has verified, rather than on potentially incorrect automated inferences. The confirmed configuration---including any user modifications---is persisted as part of the session record to support auditability and reproducibility.

#### 3.3.2 Spectral Ranking Inference

The computation engine implements spectral methods for ranking inference from multiway comparison data. We summarize the assumed choice model, the spectral estimator, and the uncertainty quantification procedure below; full theoretical treatment, including minimax optimality proofs and asymptotic analysis, is provided in Fan et al. [8].

**Choice model.** Let $n$ denote the number of items to be ranked. We model preferences through the Plackett-Luce framework [46, 47], parameterizing item quality by $\boldsymbol{\theta}^* = (\theta_1^*, \ldots, \theta_n^*)^\top$ such that for any choice set $A \subseteq [n]$ and item $i \in A$:

$$P(i \text{ wins among } A) = \frac{e^{\theta_i^*}}{\sum_{k \in A} e^{\theta_k^*}}$$

This model encompasses the Bradley-Terry-Luce model for pairwise comparisons as a special case when $|A| = 2$ [48]. The observed data consist of $L$ comparisons $\{(c_l, A_l)\}_{l=1}^L$, where $A_l$ is the choice set and $c_l \in A_l$ denotes the selected (winning) item, accommodating heterogeneous comparison structures where choice sets may vary in size [8].

**Spectral estimator.** The spectral approach constructs a Markov chain over items whose stationary distribution reflects latent preferences [49, 50]. Define two index sets for each item pair $(i, j)$: $W_j = \{l : c_l = j\}$ (comparisons won by $j$) and $L_i = \{l : i \in A_l,\, c_l \neq i\}$ (comparisons in which $i$ participated but did not win). Their intersection $W_j \cap L_i = \{l \in [L] : i, j \in A_l,\, c_l = j\}$ collects all comparisons where $i$ and $j$ co-occurred and $j$ won. The transition matrix $\mathbf{P}$ is defined with entries:

$$P_{ij} = \frac{1}{d} \sum_{l \in W_j \cap L_i} \frac{1}{f(A_l)}, \quad i \neq j; \qquad P_{ii} = 1 - \sum_{j \neq i} P_{ij}$$

where $d = 2 \cdot \max_i \sum_{l:\, i \in A_l} 1$ is a global normalizing constant (twice the maximum item participation count) and $f(A_l)$ is a weighting function that controls the relative influence of comparisons of different sizes. The diagonal entries absorb the remaining probability mass, ensuring row-stochasticity. This matrix characterizes a Markov chain whose long-run visiting frequencies reflect the underlying preference structure. The stationary distribution $\hat{\boldsymbol{\pi}}$---the eigenvector of $\mathbf{P}^\top$ associated with eigenvalue 1---serves as the spectral score for each item. Preference score estimates are obtained via the centering transformation:

$$\hat{\theta}_i = \log \hat{\pi}_i - \frac{1}{n} \sum_{k=1}^{n} \log \hat{\pi}_k$$

and the inferred ranking is produced by sorting $\hat{\theta}_i$ in descending order. Compared to maximum likelihood estimation under the Plackett-Luce model, which requires iterative optimization with potentially non-convex objectives [5, 6], the spectral estimator requires only a single eigenvalue computation, achieving the same minimax optimal convergence rates with substantially lower computational cost [8].

**Uncertainty quantification.** Confidence intervals for ranks are constructed using the Gaussian multiplier bootstrap [52], which approximates the joint sampling distribution of $\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}} - \boldsymbol{\theta}^*$ without parametric assumptions on the comparison process. For each item $i$, a variance estimator $\hat{\tau}_i$ is computed from the participation matrix and the estimated stationary distribution to capture the local curvature of the spectral map. The bootstrap procedure generates $B$ replicates by multiplying the centered influence function by i.i.d. standard normal random variables, then extracts critical values from the maximum of the standardized bootstrap statistics to account for simultaneous inference over all item pairs. The resulting integer-valued rank confidence intervals $[\hat{R}_i^L,\, \hat{R}_i^U]$ are:

$$\hat{R}_i^L = 1 + \sum_{j \neq i} \mathbf{1}\!\left\{\frac{\hat{\theta}_j - \hat{\theta}_i}{\hat{\sigma}_{ij}} > \hat{c}_{i,\,0.95}\right\}, \qquad \hat{R}_i^U = n - \sum_{j \neq i} \mathbf{1}\!\left\{\frac{\hat{\theta}_j - \hat{\theta}_i}{\hat{\sigma}_{ij}} < -\hat{c}_{i,\,0.95}\right\}$$

where $\hat{\sigma}_{ij} = \sqrt{\hat{v}_i + \hat{v}_j}$ is the estimated standard deviation of the score difference and $\hat{c}_{i,\,0.95}$ is the 95th percentile of the bootstrap distribution for item $i$. These integer-valued rank confidence intervals provide a direct statistical basis for determining whether observed rank differences are significant or attributable to sampling variability, without requiring the user to interpret continuous score differences.

#### 3.3.3 Engine Execution

After user confirmation, OmniRank invokes the spectral engine as a deterministic subprocess. The engine applies the spectral estimator described in Section 3.3.2 with constant weighting $f(A_l) \equiv 2$, producing preference score estimates $\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}$ and integer-valued rank confidence intervals via the Gaussian multiplier bootstrap [8]. Execution proceeds in four steps:

1. **Parameter assembly**: The confirmed schema, bootstrap parameters ($B$, seed), and preprocessed data path are composed into an engine configuration object.

2. **Subprocess invocation**: The R-based spectral engine executes in an isolated subprocess with timeout protection, ensuring that computation failures do not propagate to the agent's context window.

3. **Output extraction**: The engine returns structured output containing preference scores ($\hat{\theta}_i$), integer ranks, and two-sided 95\% rank confidence intervals, which are parsed into the agent's session memory.

4. **Trace logging**: Execution parameters, runtime, and diagnostic metadata (spectral gap, sparsity ratio, heterogeneity index) are recorded for reproducibility and error diagnosis.

Because engine execution is deterministic given the confirmed inputs, ranking computations are reproducible and fully isolated from the stochastic behavior of the LLM agent.

### 3.4 Result Synthesis and User Interaction

The final phase converts computational outputs into interpretable artifacts and supports ongoing exploratory analysis. The LLM agent synthesizes natural-language narratives grounded in the computed preference estimates and uncertainty quantification, while deterministic tools produce publication-quality visualizations.

#### 3.4.1 Report and Visualization Generation

Upon receiving ranking results from the engine, OmniRank generates a structured analysis report through LLM-based natural language generation. The report comprises four components:

- **Executive summary**: Key findings highlighting top-ranked items, statistically indistinguishable tiers, and notable patterns.
- **Detailed rankings**: Tabular presentation of ranks, preference scores ($\hat{\theta}_i$), and 95\% rank confidence intervals.
- **Methodology notes**: Description of the spectral approach, validation outcomes, and any non-blocking warnings (e.g., sparsity).
- **Domain-specific interpretation**: Contextual analysis tailored to the data domain, leveraging the semantic schema inferred during data processing.

OmniRank produces a complementary suite of deterministic visualizations:

1. *Forest plots*: Point estimates with confidence interval error bars, enabling visual assessment of ranking uncertainty and identification of statistically indistinguishable groups [41].
2. *Comparison heatmaps*: Matrix visualizations of pairwise win rates revealing competitive structure among items.
3. *Score distributions*: Density plots of estimated preference parameters $\hat{\theta}_i$ illustrating separation between items.

All visualizations are generated deterministically (no LLM involvement) and exported in SVG format for print-quality rendering.

#### 3.4.2 Interactive Question-Answering

OmniRank supports follow-up queries through a conversational interface, enabling users to explore results without restarting the analysis. This capability relies on a session memory architecture that maintains three categories of state across tool calls:

- **Data state**: Current schema, validation results, and confirmed configuration parameters.
- **Execution trace**: Log of engine invocations, runtime metadata, and intermediate results for error diagnosis.
- **Conversation context**: History of user queries and agent responses enabling contextual follow-up.

When a user poses a follow-up question, the agent retrieves relevant evidence from session memory before generating a response. For example, when asked "Is model A significantly better than model B?", the agent retrieves the corresponding preference scores ($\hat{\theta}_A$, $\hat{\theta}_B$) and rank confidence intervals, applies the non-overlapping confidence interval criterion, and synthesizes a statistically grounded response. This evidence-first retrieval strategy grounds responses in computed artifacts rather than free-form generation, mitigating a known failure mode of LLMs in quantitative domains where plausible-sounding but numerically incorrect answers are common [32].

### 3.5 Prompt Engineering and Knowledge Integration

The agent's behavior is governed by a single structured system prompt that encodes domain expertise through in-context learning [45], following established practices in LLM agent design [43, 44]. All prompt content resides in a single source file to ensure version-controlled consistency. The prompt comprises three layers.

**Role Specification.** Defines the agent's identity as a statistical analyst specializing in comparison data and ranking inference. This layer establishes the agent's operational scope---it processes comparison data and produces ranking results, but does not attempt general-purpose data science tasks outside this domain.

**Operational Constraints.** Specifies tool invocation protocols, output format requirements, and error handling procedures. These constraints enforce the fixed three-phase pipeline (Algorithm 1): the agent must complete data validation before requesting user confirmation, and must obtain confirmation before invoking the spectral engine. This sequencing is critical because bypassing validation could send malformed data to the R engine, while bypassing confirmation could execute computation with incorrect parameters.

**Domain Knowledge Layer.** Embeds spectral ranking expertise directly into the prompt context, enabling the LLM to reason about domain-specific concepts without fine-tuning. The embedded knowledge includes: (i) format recognition rules that map data structures to pairwise or multiway classification (Section 3.2.1); (ii) validation thresholds derived from spectral ranking theory, including the $n\log n$ sparsity bound and graph connectivity requirements [8]; (iii) confidence interval interpretation guidelines that prevent common statistical misinterpretations (e.g., treating CI overlap as a formal test of equality); and (iv) diagnostic metadata definitions (spectral gap, heterogeneity index) for contextualizing engine output. This static knowledge layer provides deterministic access to domain context at every inference step, avoiding the latency and retrieval noise of dynamic knowledge-fetching approaches.

### 4.2 Comparison with Generic LLM Agents

#### 4.2.1 Task Design

**Tasks.** We constructed [12] ranking tasks spanning both format types (pairwise and multiway, including heterogeneous comparison structures) and four domains (sports, clinical trials, consumer products, and LLM evaluation). Each task provides a comparison dataset, a natural language prompt (e.g., "Rank these treatments by efficacy and provide confidence intervals"), and a ground-truth ranking computed offline using the Fan et al. [8] spectral method. Task complexity ranges from [5] items with [20] comparisons to [30] items with [500+] comparisons. All datasets were drawn from publicly available sources or synthetically generated.

**Baselines.** We compared OmniRank against four baselines, each representing a distinct paradigm for LLM-based ranking:

- *GPT-5-mini (direct)*: The same backbone model used by OmniRank, prompted to perform ranking inference directly without tool access. This baseline isolates the contribution of tool calling.
- *GPT-5 (direct)*: A more capable model prompted identically, testing whether model scale alone compensates for the absence of specialized tools.
- *DS-Agent* [54]: An automated data science agent that augments LLM code generation with case-based reasoning, retrieving relevant expert solutions from a curated knowledge base to guide its analytical workflow (Guo et al., ICML 2024). This baseline tests whether domain-general expert knowledge from machine learning competitions can compensate for the absence of spectral ranking tools.
- *SciToolAgent* [33]: A knowledge-graph-driven scientific agent that orchestrates hundreds of specialized computational tools across scientific domains via graph-based retrieval-augmented generation (Ding et al., Nature Computational Science 2025). As a multi-tool scientific agent, this baseline tests whether a general tool-calling architecture---the paradigm closest to OmniRank---can handle ranking inference when its tool registry lacks spectral ranking capabilities.

For each baseline, we supplied the same dataset and natural language instruction under default configurations. All experiments were repeated [5] times; we report means and standard deviations.

#### 4.2.2 Evaluation Protocol

**Metrics.** We evaluated outputs along four dimensions: (i) *ranking correctness*, measured by Kendall's $\tau$ between predicted and ground-truth orderings; (ii) *task completion*, the fraction of tasks producing a valid, parsable ranking; (iii) *statistical rigor*, whether the output included any uncertainty quantification (confidence intervals, p-values, or bootstrap variance); and (iv) *methodological validity*, whether the agent applied a statistically principled ranking method rather than ad hoc heuristics (e.g., sorting by aggregate win count).

**OmniRank achieves near-optimal ranking accuracy where generic agents fail.** OmniRank achieved a mean Kendall's $\tau$ of [0.96] (Table 3), substantially outperforming the best baseline (GPT-5 direct, $\tau$ = [0.61]). Notably, agent systems with code execution (DS-Agent, $\tau$ = [0.57]) or multi-tool orchestration (SciToolAgent, $\tau$ = [0.52]) did not outperform raw GPT-5, indicating that domain-general tools and knowledge bases provide no advantage for spectral ranking. On multiway tasks with more than [15] items, all baselines resort to sorting by aggregate win counts or Elo-style heuristics---approaches that ignore comparison structure and yield rankings inconsistent with the maximum likelihood estimator. By delegating computation to the spectral engine, OmniRank avoids these failure modes entirely.

**Tool calling eliminates hallucinated computations.** All four baselines exhibited at least one instance of fabricated statistical output: invented confidence intervals, nonsensical p-values, or references to methods not actually applied. OmniRank's outputs, by contrast, are deterministic given the same input data, because spectral computation is executed by a verified R script rather than generated by the LLM. This distinction is critical for scientific applications where reproducibility is non-negotiable.

**Statistical rigor requires domain-specific tools.** DS-Agent [54]---despite its knowledge base of expert analytical patterns---produced valid uncertainty quantification in only [2] of [12] tasks, and SciToolAgent [33] managed [1] of [12]. Both agents generated point estimates via ad hoc heuristics but did not implement the Gaussian multiplier bootstrap [52] required for rank confidence intervals; their tool registries and knowledge bases simply do not cover spectral ranking methodology. OmniRank produces two-sided confidence intervals for every item ranking by default, because this capability is built into the spectral engine rather than left to LLM-generated code.

**Failure modes of generic agents.** Examining the baseline runs that produced incorrect or incomplete rankings, we identified three recurring patterns: (i) *methodological substitution*, where agents applied ad hoc heuristics (win-count sorting, Elo ratings, or simple averaging) despite being prompted for ranking with uncertainty quantification; (ii) *hallucinated uncertainty quantification*, where agents generated confidence intervals or p-values without performing any underlying statistical computation; and (iii) *data misinterpretation*, where agents misidentified the comparison structure or inverted the preference direction. OmniRank's architecture addresses each pattern by design: the spectral engine prevents (i) and (ii), while the schema inference pipeline (Section 4.1) mitigates (iii).

### 5.0 Ranking of Formula 1 Drivers Across Heterogeneous Circuit Types

Formula 1 (F1) is the premier single-seater motor racing championship, in which ten constructor teams each field two drivers across a calendar of Grand Prix events held on circuits worldwide. Ranking drivers by ability is a central concern for teams, media, and governing bodies, yet the official FIA World Drivers' Championship determines this ranking through a points system that awards points to the top-ten finishers in each race and accumulates them over the season. While the points system is simple and transparent, it has well-known limitations for statistical ranking inference: it discards ordinal information from lower-finishing positions, assigns non-uniform weights to the top positions via a fixed allocation schedule that the FIA has revised multiple times across regulatory eras, and---most critically for our purposes---does not produce any form of uncertainty quantification for the resulting standings. Furthermore, direct cross-season comparison of driver ability is complicated by regulation changes, car performance differences across constructors, and mid-season roster changes that alter the composition of the competitive field from race to race. Each Grand Prix naturally constitutes a multiway comparison among the participating drivers, producing a rank-position matrix with variable subset sizes---precisely the data structure addressed by the spectral ranking inference framework [8]. This makes F1 race classification data a natural application for OmniRank, one in which the platform can produce uncertainty-aware rankings that complement the official standings by quantifying the statistical precision of the inferred driver ordering.

We apply OmniRank to race finishing positions from 48 Grand Prix events spanning the 2024 and 2025 FIA Formula 1 World Championship seasons. The dataset covers 27 distinct drivers across 10 constructors, though not all drivers participate in every race: mid-season driver replacements (e.g., reserve drivers substituting for injured or departing regulars) and roster turnover between seasons produce a naturally sparse multiway comparison structure in which each race ranks a variable-sized subset of the 27 drivers (19--20 per race, with a mean of 19.96). Finishing positions from the official race classification serve as the performance metric, with lower values indicating superior performance (position 1 = race winner); drivers classified as retired, lapped, or disqualified retain their official classification positions. A track-type categorical indicator---permanent circuits (e.g., Bahrain, Monza; 32 races), street circuits (e.g., Monaco, Singapore, Baku; 10 races), and temporary non-street circuits (e.g., Melbourne, Miami; 6 races)---enables stratified analysis to assess whether relative driver performance varies across circuit configurations that impose different aerodynamic, braking, and overtaking demands.

Upon uploading the CSV file, OmniRank's data processing phase (Phase 1) reads the dataset and infers a semantic schema: the agent identifies the multiway comparison format---each row represents a race finishing order among a subset of the 27 drivers---and infers that lower values indicate superior performance (bigbetter = 0). The agent detects the ``track\_type'' column as a categorical indicator suitable for stratified analysis, enumerating three values: permanent, street, and temporary\_non\_street. Format validation passes without errors; quality validation confirms that the comparison graph is connected across all 27 drivers and reports no sparsity warnings. In the interactive configuration phase (Phase 2), OmniRank presents these inferred parameters for user review. The user confirms the schema and default statistical settings ($B = 2{,}000$; seed = 42), and the spectral engine executes in 0.91 seconds, producing preference score estimates $\hat{\theta}_i$, integer-valued ranks, and two-sided 95\% rank confidence intervals for all 27 drivers. In the result synthesis phase (Phase 3), OmniRank generates a natural-language report and three complementary visualizations: a confidence interval forest plot showing overall ranks with CIs, a normalized ranking distribution plot displaying per-track-type rank distributions, and a track-type ranking heatmap providing the full driver-by-track-type rank matrix. The entire pipeline---from data upload through report delivery---completes in under two minutes without requiring the user to write any code or interact with the R spectral engine directly.

### 5.1 Ranking of Polygenic Risk Score Training Methods

Polygenic risk scores (PRS), also commonly referred to as polygenic scores (PGS), aggregate the effects of hundreds to millions of single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) into a single measure of genetic liability for a phenotype. Due to limited access to individual-level genotype data and privacy concerns, current PRS training methods largely rely on GWAS summary statistics and linkage disequilibrium (LD) reference panels. Over the past decade, more than 30 methods have been proposed for PRS construction in this setting, ranging from simple clumping-and-thresholding approaches (C+T) to Bayesian shrinkage methods (e.g., LDpred2, PRS-CS, SBayesR) and penalized regression approaches (e.g., lassosum2). Individual method papers typically benchmark their proposed approach against a subset of existing methods on a limited set of phenotypes, and subsequent application studies evaluate different subsets across broader phenotype ranges. The resulting benchmarking evidence is fragmented: no single study covers all methods on all phenotypes, performance metrics (R-squared, AUC, partial correlation) are not directly comparable across studies, and the comparison tables are sparse with many missing entries. This data structure---heterogeneous multiway comparisons with varying subset sizes---is precisely the regime addressed by the spectral ranking inference framework [8], making it a natural application for OmniRank.

We demonstrate OmniRank's full pipeline on a benchmarking dataset derived from 35 PubMed-indexed publications (2009--2025) that reported comparative performance of 14 GWAS summary data-based PRS training methods: C+T, SCT, LDpred, LDpred2, LDpred2-auto, LDpred2-inf, LDpred-funct, AnnoPred, lassosum, lassosum2, PRS-CS, PRS-CS-auto, SBayesR, and DBSLMM (citation needed). The dataset contains 329 comparison records spanning 24 phenotypes, including binary clinical outcomes (e.g., breast cancer, type 2 diabetes, schizophrenia) and continuous physiological traits (e.g., body mass index, height, cholesterol). Each record benchmarks a heterogeneous subset of the 14 methods on a specific phenotype from a single reference paper: some records compare as few as 3 methods while others compare all 14, producing a sparse multiway comparison structure with varying hyperedge sizes.

Upon uploading the CSV file, OmniRank's data processing phase (Phase 1) reads the dataset and infers a semantic schema: the agent identifies the multiway comparison format---each row represents a comparison among a subset of the 14 methods---and infers that higher metric values indicate superior performance (bigbetter = 1). The agent further detects the ``Phenotype'' column as a categorical indicator suitable for stratified analysis, enumerating all 24 phenotype values. Data validation confirms that the comparison graph is connected and that the comparison count is sufficient for reliable spectral inference, permitting an overall ranking with no blocking errors. In the interactive configuration phase (Phase 2), OmniRank presents these inferred parameters for user review. The user confirms the schema and default statistical settings ($B = 2{,}000$; seed = 42), and the spectral engine executes, producing preference score estimates $\hat{\theta}_i$, integer-valued ranks, and two-sided 95\% rank confidence intervals for all 14 methods. In the result synthesis phase (Phase 3), OmniRank generates a natural-language report and three complementary visualizations: a confidence interval forest plot showing overall ranks with CIs, a normalized ranking distribution plot displaying per-phenotype rank distributions, and a phenotype ranking heatmap providing the full method-by-phenotype rank matrix. The platform then opens an interactive question-answering session grounded in the computed results. The entire pipeline---from data upload through report delivery---completes in under two minutes without requiring the user to write any code or interact with the R spectral engine directly.

This aligns with known methodological characteristics: C+T is the simplest clumping-and-thresholding baseline, and LDpred2-inf is a deliberately simplified infinite-shrinkage variant of LDpred2 with a narrower modeling scope.

**Phenotype-stratified rankings and confidence interval widths across genetic architectures.** In the phenotype-stratified analysis, OmniRank recomputes the spectral ranking within each of the 24 phenotypes independently. The normalized ranking distribution plot ([Figure: Normalized Ranking Over Individual Phenotypes]) displays the per-phenotype rank spread for each method: even LDpred2, the overall top-ranked method, does not dominate uniformly and exhibits per-phenotype ranks spanning the full range depending on the trait. The phenotype ranking heatmap ([Figure: Phenotype Rankings]) provides a method-by-phenotype rank matrix that exposes this heterogeneity at full granularity. For phenotypes with dense comparison sets---such as Depression (39 comparisons), Schizophrenia (38), Type 2 Diabetes (28), and Height (23)---the per-phenotype ordering is more stable. Conversely, phenotypes with sparser comparison sets (e.g., Intelligence with 2 comparisons, Multiple Sclerosis with 3) yield less stable per-phenotype ranks. These phenotype-specific patterns reflect known dependencies of PRS method performance on genetic architecture: the relative performance of PRS methods depends on how well each method's distributional assumption approximates the true effect-size distribution of genetic variants for a given phenotype. For instance, methods assuming a sparse genetic architecture may excel for phenotypes with a few large-effect variants but underperform for highly polygenic traits such as schizophrenia or depression, where causal variants are widespread across the genome. Researchers can consult the phenotype-specific rankings alongside the overall ranking to identify methods most promising for their target phenotype.

**Interactive Q\&A grounds follow-up analysis in computed confidence intervals.** After report generation, users can pose follow-up questions through OmniRank's conversational interface without restarting the analysis. For instance, when asked ``Is LDpred2 significantly better than AnnoPred?'', OmniRank retrieves the relevant confidence intervals from the session memory: LDpred2's CI is [1, 2] while AnnoPred's is [1, 9]. Because AnnoPred's CI includes LDpred2's point-estimate rank, the system reports that the evidence does not support a statistically significant difference at the 95\% level---despite a gap of 0.32 in preference scores. When asked ``Which method has the lowest CI lower bound?'', OmniRank retrieves the full CI table and reports that C+T (CI [12, 14]) has the highest CI lower bound at 12, placing it below the overlapping-CI band of the middle five methods. These responses are derived from the computed spectral estimates and bootstrap confidence intervals---rather than free-form LLM generation---avoiding the hallucination risk inherent in standard LLM arithmetic while remaining accessible to domain experts who may not be versed in spectral ranking theory.

### 5.2 Ranking of LLM in head-to-head Arena

Evaluating and ranking large language models (LLMs) has become a central concern as the number of competing models grows rapidly. Two prominent public data sources drive this evaluation. The LMSYS Chatbot Arena [1] collects blind head-to-head battles in which users vote for the better response between two anonymous models, producing pairwise comparison outcomes. The Hugging Face Open LLM Leaderboard reports benchmark scores for the top 100 open models across six standardized evaluations, producing pointwise performance data. Both datasets power widely followed leaderboards, yet both publish only point-estimate rankings (Elo ratings or sorted averages) without uncertainty quantification---leaving users unable to determine whether apparent rank differences are statistically meaningful or artifacts of sampling variability. We apply OmniRank to both datasets, demonstrating how the platform handles pairwise and pointwise comparison formats and surfaces statistical structure that existing leaderboards cannot express.

#### 5.2.1 Preference-Based Ranking from LMSYS Chatbot Arena

We upload to OmniRank an Arena snapshot covering 52 models and seven task categories: Creative Writing, Math, Instruction Following, Coding, Hard Prompt, Longer Query, and Multi-turn. In the data processing phase (Phase 1), OmniRank's schema inference tool automatically identifies the pairwise comparison format---each row records a head-to-head battle between two models with a binary winner/loser outcome---and detects the task category column as a categorical indicator suitable for stratified analysis. The agent infers that a win indicates superior performance (bigbetter = 1). Data validation confirms graph connectivity across all 52 models and reports sufficient comparison data for reliable spectral inference, with no warnings issued. In the interactive configuration phase (Phase 2), OmniRank presents the inferred schema---pairwise format, 52 items, 7 indicator values---for user confirmation. The user accepts the defaults and the spectral engine executes, computing preference score estimates $\hat{\theta}_i$ and two-sided 95\% rank confidence intervals for all 52 models. In the result synthesis phase (Phase 3), OmniRank generates a report containing an overall ranking (pooling all categories), stratified rankings per category, and a CI forest plot, then opens the interactive Q\&A session.

**Overall preference scores and rank confidence intervals for 52 Arena models.** In the pooled analysis across all seven categories, the spectral ranking places gemini-2.5-pro-preview-03-25 at rank 1 (CI [1, 2]) and gemini-2.5-pro at rank 2 (CI [1, 2]). The overlapping confidence intervals indicate that the available head-to-head evidence does not support separating these two models at the 95\% confidence level---a distinction that the Arena's Elo-based leaderboard, which reports a single point-estimate ordering, cannot express. The next group includes grok-4-0709 (rank 3; CI [3, 9]) and chatgpt-4o-latest-20250326 (rank 4; CI [3, 7]), with overlapping intervals that reflect substantial uncertainty among models beyond the top pair.

**Category-stratified rankings and confidence interval widths across seven task types.** Using the detected indicator column, OmniRank recomputes the spectral ranking within each of the seven task categories. The top-ranked model varies by category: gemini-2.5-pro-preview-03-25 is placed first in Creative Writing (CI [1, 9]), Hard Prompt (CI [1, 6]), and Instruction Following (CI [1, 4]); gemini-2.5-pro is placed first in Coding (CI [1, 4]) and Math (CI [1, 6]); and chatgpt-4o-latest-20250326 is placed first in Multi-turn (CI [1, 8]). #### 5.2.2 Benchmark-Based Ranking from the Hugging Face Open LLM Leaderboard

We further apply OmniRank to the Hugging Face Open LLM Leaderboard, which reports scores for the top 100 open models across six benchmarks: IFEval, BBH (Big Bench Hard), MATH, GPQA (Graduate-Level Google-Proof Q\&A), MuSR (Multistep Soft Reasoning), and MMLU-Pro. Data validation flags a non-blocking sparsity warning: with only 6 comparisons across 100 models, the data falls below the sample complexity threshold required for minimax optimal inference, so confidence intervals may be wider than theoretically optimal. ## 6 Conclusion

We have presented OmniRank, an agentic framework that provides a natural language interface to spectral ranking inference from arbitrary multiway comparisons. By decoupling semantic understanding from mathematical computation---an LLM agent interprets data and synthesizes results while a deterministic spectral engine performs all numerical operations---OmniRank preserves the minimax optimality and bootstrap-based uncertainty quantification of the underlying spectral method [8] while removing the linear algebra and programming expertise that have confined it to specialist use.

Experimental results validate this architecture across multiple evaluation dimensions. The LLM-based schema inference tool achieves high accuracy on standard comparison formats and degrades gracefully on ambiguous inputs, where the human-in-the-loop confirmation step serves as a safety net. The deterministic validation pipeline catches all structural defects without false negatives. In end-to-end comparison against generic LLM agents, OmniRank attains a mean Kendall's $\tau$ of [0.96]---substantially exceeding all baselines, including GPT-5 without specialized tools ($\tau$ = [0.61])---and is the only system that produces valid bootstrap confidence intervals in every task. Case studies on Formula 1 driver ranking, PRS training method benchmarking, the LMSYS Chatbot Arena, and the Hugging Face Open LLM Leaderboard demonstrate that OmniRank surfaces statistical structure---statistically indistinguishable tiers, context-dependent performance variation, and the precision limits imposed by small comparison sets---that point-estimate leaderboards cannot express.

Several directions remain open. Schema inference on structurally ambiguous inputs can produce silently incorrect schemas that bypass the confirmation checkpoint; incorporating active learning from user corrections is a natural path to reducing these failures. The system currently supports only spectral inference under the Plackett-Luce model; extending the engine to accommodate Bayesian preference models, regularized MLE, and partial ranking methods would broaden applicability. The Gaussian multiplier bootstrap requires sufficient sample complexity ($M \geq cn\log n$); while OmniRank's built-in sparsity diagnostics already flag datasets below this threshold, future versions could offer alternative inference procedures for sparse regimes. Prospective user studies across multiple disciplines would provide evidence on OmniRank's impact on time-to-insight and decision quality beyond the controlled evaluations reported here.

By bridging spectral ranking theory and the practical needs of domain experts in the social and natural sciences, OmniRank demonstrates that the tool-calling paradigm---where LLMs orchestrate but do not execute precise computation---can make statistically rigorous inference accessible to practitioners who describe their comparison data in natural language. As the range of supported ranking models and data modalities expands, this architecture can serve as a template for democratizing other specialized statistical methods that currently require domain-specific programming expertise.
